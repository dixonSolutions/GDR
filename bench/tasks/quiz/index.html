The ochre line: how Vellmar lost its trams

City archive feature · 6 minute read

The Vellmar Tramway opened on 14 March 1908, three years after the city council rejected a proposal for an underground railway on the grounds that the river silt would make tunnelling ruinous. The surface network that replaced the plan was cheaper, slower, and — as it turned out — far more durable in the public memory.

Its chief engineer was Anselm Brecht, a Bremen-trained bridge designer who had never built a railway of any kind. Brecht's inexperience produced the network's most distinctive feature: rather than radiating from a central terminus, the Vellmar lines formed a rough figure-eight, crossing themselves twice at the Salt Market. Later engineers called the layout inefficient. Passengers called it convenient, because it meant almost every district connected to almost every other without a change.

The network grew steadily through the 1920s. At its peak in 1931 it operated 47 stations across six routes, carrying roughly 90,000 passengers a day in a city of 210,000 people. Each route was assigned a colour, painted on the car's front panel so that passengers who could not read timetables could still find their line. The busiest of these, running from the shipyards to the university, was the ochre route — a colour chosen, according to Brecht's notes, simply because the paint was cheap and did not fade in salt air.

Decline came slowly and then all at once. Buses took the outer routes in the 1950s. The council debated closure four times without deciding. What settled the argument was the flood of 1962, which put the Salt Market crossing under two metres of water and destroyed the signalling huts at the centre of the figure-eight. Repair was costed at more than the network had earned in the previous decade. The last car ran that November.

Almost nothing survives. The rails were lifted for scrap within two years, and the depot became a supermarket car park. One ochre car was kept, and now sits in the transport museum at Halden, repainted — to the irritation of local historians — in a green livery the Vellmar Tramway never used.
